# Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)

People don't buy products; they **hire** them to make progress in a situation. Features are how;
the *job* is why. This skill keeps the conversation on the job.

## The core question

> "What progress was the customer trying to make, in what circumstance, and what was getting in
> the way?"

A job is stable over time; the solutions that serve it change. Frame the job as
**verb + object + context**, solution-free: *"help me feel confident I won't miss a deadline while
juggling five projects"* — not *"a better calendar."*

## The three dimensions of every job

For any job, name all three — most teams only see the first:

1. **Functional** — the practical task ("track my spending").
2. **Emotional** — how the person wants to feel ("not anxious about money").
3. **Social** — how they want to be seen ("responsible to my family").

If a competitor wins on emotional/social while you win on functional, you lose.

## The four forces of progress

Every switch is a tug-of-war. Diagnose all four before proposing anything:

- **Push** of the current situation (what's frustrating now)
- **Pull** of the new solution (the promise of the better way)
- **Anxiety** about the new solution (fear, risk, learning cost) — *the silent deal-killer*
- **Habit** of the present (inertia, sunk cost)

Progress happens only when **Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit**. Most roadmaps over-invest in Pull
(more features) and ignore Anxiety and Habit — which is where switches actually stall.

## Switch interviews (how to discover the job)

Interview people who **recently switched** (to you or away from you). Walk the timeline backward
from the purchase:

1. **First thought** — when did they first realize the old way wasn't working?
2. **Passive looking** — what nudged them to start considering alternatives?
3. **Active looking** — what triggered a real search? What did they compare?
4. **Deciding** — what almost stopped them (the anxieties)? What tipped it?
5. **First use** — did it deliver the progress they hired it for?

Ask "walk me through the day you decided," not "what features do you want." Chase the *story and
the emotion*, not opinions about your product.

## Write the job statement

After interviews, capture each job as:

> **When** [situation], **I want to** [motivation/progress], **so I can** [desired outcome].

Example: *"When I'm handed a repo I've never seen, I want to understand how it fits together
without reading every file, so I can make a change without breaking something."*

## The "Little Hire / Big Hire" churn lens

- **Big Hire** = they bought/adopted it.
- **Little Hire** = they actually used it for the job.
Churn is usually a string of failed Little Hires. If people adopt but don't return, the job isn't
getting done — look at the first-use moment, not the sales funnel.

## How to use this skill

1. State the job as verb + object + context, solution-free.
2. Name the functional, emotional, and social dimensions.
3. Map the four forces — especially Anxiety and Habit.
4. If motivation is unclear, draft switch-interview questions before building anything.
5. Turn findings into job statements that feed prioritization and positioning.

Pairs with `hook-model` (design the habit loop once the job is clear) and `cro-objections`
(remove the anxieties that block the switch at conversion).
